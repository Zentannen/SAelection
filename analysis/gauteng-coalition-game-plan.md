# Gauteng after the 2026 local elections: a scenario game plan

*Written on 5 November 2026, the day after the vote, with counting still under way. The IEC is due to declare results on Sunday 8 November. This is scenario planning, not prediction: it sets out what could happen, why, what would cause each outcome, and how each one could fall apart.*

---

## How this plan was built

Three inputs, in this order of weight:

1. **What parties actually believe.** Economic policy, race-based redress, respect for the constitutional order, immigration and law-and-order. Parties that believe similar things can govern together for longer.
2. **What parties need.** Office, patronage, survival, their next internal leadership contest, their 2029 national ambitions, their brand with their own voters.
3. **The numbers.** Which combinations cross the seat thresholds in each council, and who is pivotal.

**What politicians say about who they will and won't work with is treated as an opening bid, not a fact.** South African councils have taught that lesson repeatedly:

- ActionSA said in 2021 it would not work with the ANC "not even in 300 years". In 2024 it took the Tshwane mayoralty with ANC votes.
- The EFF voted DA mayors into office in 2016 and 2021, put ANC and Al Jama-ah mayors in office in 2022–23, and in 2026 took the Gauteng finance portfolio under an ANC premier.
- The DA campaigned for years against the ANC and has sat in the ANC's national cabinet since 2024.
- The Patriotic Alliance has governed with both the ANC and the DA, sometimes in the same year.

Statements matter only where they reflect a real belief gap or a real cost with the party's own voters. The plan tries to separate those from posturing.

**Game theory is used as a tool for thinking, not as proof.** It explains why a party with a few seats can extract the mayoralty, or why a threat is or isn't believable. It doesn't capture factions, personal grudges, secret-ballot defections or money changing hands, and in South African councils those often decide the vote. Where the theory and the politics disagree, the politics wins.

---

## 1. The bottom line

1. **The question that decides Gauteng is whether the DA and ActionSA together cross half the seats** in Johannesburg (136 of 270), Ekurhuleni (113 of about 224) and Tshwane (108 of 214). On a sober reading of the count (the early DA lead will shrink as township districts report), Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni look set to land close to that line, within a handful of seats either side. Tshwane is a three-way split between the DA, ActionSA and the ANC.

2. **If the DA and ActionSA cross that line, they will most likely govern together.** That's not because their leaders like each other; they don't. They believe more of the same things than any other two large parties (market economics, anti-corruption, constitutional government, tough immigration enforcement), and neither has a better offer. **The fight will be over price**: who wears the mayoral chain, which portfolios ActionSA gets, and whether the DA keeps Helen Zille as its Johannesburg face.

3. **ActionSA's price is set by the ANC, not by Herman Mashaba.** ActionSA can only demand the mayoralty if it has a credible alternative: an ANC-backed coalition in which ActionSA wears the chain, as in Tshwane in 2024. If ANC national leadership stops its Gauteng structures from making that offer, ActionSA's leverage collapses and it settles as a junior partner. **So the DA's real counterpart in negotiations over ActionSA is Luthuli House.**

4. **If the DA and ActionSA fall short, national politics decides the fallback.** If the ANC's national leadership (Ramaphosa, Mbalula and the top seven) enforces the logic of the national unity government, the fallback is an **ANC–DA grand coalition**. If the Gauteng ANC (the Lesufi/Mashatile line) runs its own talks, the fallback is an **anti-DA bloc** of ANC + ActionSA + EFF + small parties.

5. **The provincial government is not on the ballot, but it is in play.** Premier Panyaza Lesufi's ANC has 28 of 80 seats and governs only because the EFF sits inside his executive (the EFF holds the finance portfolio). Every metro where the ANC partners the DA strains that arrangement. Every metro where the ANC is pushed into opposition binds it closer to the EFF.

6. **The most likely Gauteng-wide end-state is "cohabitation"**: DA-led governments, with ActionSA, in Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni; a contested Tshwane; and an ANC–EFF provincial government in opposition to all of them. That tension (over interventions in municipalities, grants, policing and housing) would be the central political story in Gauteng until 2029.

7. **The first mayor elected is not necessarily the government that lasts.** In 2021, the first mayoral votes reflected parties' public positions. Within one to three years, every Gauteng metro had moved to the arrangement that beliefs and numbers pointed to. Treat the November 2026 mayoral votes as round one.

## 2. The rules that shape every scenario

- **What was elected.** Councils: the three metros (Johannesburg, Tshwane, Ekurhuleni), plus Emfuleni, Midvaal, Lesedi, Mogale City, Rand West, Merafong and the Sedibeng and West Rand district councils. The Gauteng provincial legislature was elected in 2024 and isn't up again until 2029.
- **Seats follow the proportional (PR) ballot.** Winning wards doesn't add seats; it changes which of a party's seats are ward seats and which are list seats. A party's share of council seats closely tracks its PR vote.
- **The 14-day clock.** Councils must hold their first sitting within 14 days of results being declared, so roughly by 22 November. Mayors and speakers are elected there. Europe can take months (Belgium once took 541 days); South Africa forces half-finished deals, which then unravel.
- **Three thresholds, not one:**
  - **Electing the mayor.** Secret ballot, decided by a majority of the votes *cast*, with the weakest candidate eliminated round by round. **A party can win the mayoralty without a majority coalition** if rivals abstain or split. The DA won Johannesburg and Tshwane this way in 2016 and 2021, on EFF votes and with no EFF deal.
  - **Passing a budget.** Needs a majority of *all* councillors. A minority mayor can be elected but can't govern for long without a budget majority.
  - **Removing a mayor.** A motion of no confidence also needs a majority of all councillors. The parties voting to remove a mayor only need to agree on removal, not on a replacement. That "negative majority" is how Johannesburg changed mayor five times between late 2022 and 2024.
- **The coalition law is not yet in force.** The Municipal Structures Amendment Bill (motions of no confidence limited to once a year, coalition agreements made public) stalled before the election and will only resume afterwards. For the first months of this term, councils run on the old, unstable rules. If the bill passes quickly, whatever is formed in November gets locked in for a year. That raises the stakes of the first deal.

## 3. Where the count stands and how to read it

Early figures (roughly 10–25% of voting districts counted on 5 November) showed:

- **Johannesburg:** DA around 40%, ANC and ActionSA each in the mid-to-high teens, EFF around 6%.
- **Ekurhuleni:** DA around 37–42%, ActionSA and the ANC each in the mid-to-high teens.
- **Tshwane:** ActionSA narrowly ahead (mid-20s to about 30%), the ANC close behind, the DA around 20%.
- **Gauteng overall** (about a fifth counted): DA 38, ANC 20, ActionSA 15, EFF 6, MK 4.

**Why not take this at face value:** in South African metros, smaller suburban districts report first and favour the DA. Large township districts, where the ANC, EFF and MK are stronger, report later. Pre-election polls were much tighter: Ipsos had Johannesburg at DA 30 / ANC 27 / EFF 12 / ActionSA 11 / MK 5 and Tshwane at DA 34 / ANC 31, while eNCA–MarkData had Johannesburg at DA 35 / ANC 22.

**Sensible expectations for the final count:** the DA ahead in Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni but in the 30s rather than at 40%; the ANC around 20% (its worst Gauteng result ever); ActionSA in the teens and much stronger than in 2021; the EFF in single digits, well down; MK small in Gauteng. Tshwane remains genuinely three-way. Two things stand out:

- **The ANC, for the first time, is a junior bidder in Gauteng's councils.** That changes its behaviour more than anything else in this election (section 5).
- **The EFF's collapse costs it its kingmaker role in the councils.** That pushes its leverage to the one place it still has it: the provincial legislature.

## 4. A game-theory toolkit, and where each tool breaks

These are the concepts used throughout the scenarios, in plain terms.

| Tool | What it says | How it applies here | Where it breaks in SA councils |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pivotal player (kingmaker)** | A party's power comes from being the one whose seats turn a losing combination into a winning one, not from its size | ActionSA is pivotal in all three metros. Small parties (FF+, PA, IFP, ACDP, Al Jama-ah) become pivotal where a majority sits on a knife-edge | Pivotal parties often overplay and get cut out, or their councillors defect in the secret ballot |
| **Outside option** (what you get if you walk away) | Your price in negotiations is set by your best alternative | ActionSA can only demand the chain if an ANC-backed coalition will give it one. The DA can only refuse ActionSA's demands if it has another route to a majority, such as the ANC or small parties | Alternatives exist on paper but are often blocked by factions or personal hatred |
| **Credible vs empty threats** | A threat only works if carrying it out is in the threatener's interest | Zille's "we'd rather be in opposition than depend on the EFF" is credible because a DA–EFF arrangement would cost the DA heavily with its voters. Mashaba's "remove Zille" is less credible because walking away leaves ActionSA with nothing in Joburg | Leaders sometimes carry out costly threats for internal reasons (to hold their party together) |
| **Cheap talk** | Statements that cost nothing to make change little | Pre-election "we will never work with X" is cheap talk unless it tracks a real belief gap | Some statements become costly once voters remember them, so they partly bind |
| **Minimum winning coalitions** | Parties prefer the smallest coalition that wins, so each gets more of the spoils | Expect lean coalitions (DA + ActionSA + one small party), not grand ones, unless something else forces it | In SA, a larger buffer is valuable because councillors defect; the very thin coalitions of 2021–24 were destroyed by single defections |
| **Connected coalitions** | Coalitions form between parties next to each other on policy, without skipping over a party in between | DA–ActionSA–FF+ and ANC–EFF–PA hang together; DA–EFF or ActionSA–EFF inside one government skips huge gaps | "Anyone but X" coalitions skip gaps for a while (Tshwane 2024), but they're fragile |
| **Two-level games** | Leaders negotiate at two tables at once, with each other and with their own side, and each table constrains the other | Ramaphosa and Hill-Lewis bargain nationally, but each must sell the deal to Gauteng structures. Lesufi's provincial deal with the EFF constrains what the ANC can offer the DA in the metros | Factions can defect openly rather than just constrain the leader |
| **Commitment problems** | Deals collapse when a party can't credibly promise not to defect later | The 2024 ActionSA–DA split in Tshwane makes promises between them hard to believe, so expect demands for written agreements and national-level guarantees | Written agreements in SA councils have rarely been enforced |
| **Repeated games and reputation** | Parties that will meet again behave better | The DA and ActionSA both face 2029; burning each other now costs both | Short horizons (the ANC's 2027 conference, individual councillors' careers) beat long ones |
| **Chicken** (who swerves first) | Two sides each threaten to crash the deal; whoever looks more willing to crash wins | DA vs ActionSA over the Joburg chain, under the 14-day clock | Sometimes neither swerves and the council gets a minority mayor by default |

## 5. The actors: beliefs, needs, and likely behaviour

### Democratic Alliance (leader Geordin Hill-Lewis since April 2026; federal chair and Gauteng leader Solly Msimanga; Johannesburg candidate Helen Zille)

- **Believes:** market economics, race-neutral policy (opposes race-based quotas and expropriation without compensation), constitutional supremacy, clean administration, devolution; harder on illegal immigration than it used to be.
- **Needs:** a Gauteng success story before 2029. Hill-Lewis's stated goal is to lead the national government by 2029, and the Cape Town model is his whole brand. Johannesburg is the prize, and also a trap: decaying water and power infrastructure, weak finances, failing municipal entities.
- **Constraints:** the memory of 2016–2022, when DA-led coalitions collapsed and the party was blamed. Its voters dislike both ANC deals and dependence on the EFF. Zille is popular with the base but deeply divisive with ActionSA: her 2019 return to DA leadership is what drove Mashaba out as Joburg mayor.
- **Says:** it would rather go to opposition than run a coalition dependent on the EFF.
- **Will probably do:** pay ActionSA a real price, including the Tshwane chain if it must. Bring in small partners (FF+, ACDP, BOSA, COPE, IFP). Keep the EFF out of government, because the belief gap is genuine and its voters would punish it. Quietly accept EFF votes in a secret ballot if offered, but not build a government on them. Consider the ANC only through a national-level deal, and only as the senior partner.
- **Walk-away point:** being junior to the ANC in Johannesburg, or any arrangement where the EFF decides whether it survives.

### African National Congress (talks centralised in the national top seven; Gauteng led by Lesufi; mayoral candidates deliberately not named)

- **Believes:** a developmental state, race-based redress (BBBEE), a broad-church nationalist movement. In practice, at local level, it is the most office-seeking party in the country. Municipal entities, procurement and jobs sustain its local networks.
- **Needs:** not to be shut out of all three Gauteng metros; to protect its networks; to stay united before its December 2027 national conference; not to wreck the national unity government.
- **Constraints:** **it is split.** One wing, close to the national leadership, wants a DA deal for stability and to protect the GNU. The other, around Lesufi and endorsed by Deputy President Mashatile, wants the ANC–EFF "Gauteng template" extended, possibly with ActionSA, possibly even with MK. Its 20% result means it can't lead anything without paying.
- **Says:** it wants majorities, not coalitions; only the top seven negotiate; mayoral candidates come after the results.
- **Will probably do:** whatever its winning faction decides, and that is the biggest unknown in Gauteng. Its strongest play is *kingmaker*: offer ActionSA the mayoralty (as in Tshwane 2024) to get back into government as the second-largest partner. Its other play is *grand bargain*: offer the DA stability in exchange for mayoral-committee seats. A third option, deliberately choosing opposition to rebuild, is rational but culturally alien to the ANC.
- **Walk-away point:** being a minor partner with no meaningful portfolios.

### ActionSA (leader and Johannesburg candidate Herman Mashaba; Tshwane mayor Nasiphi Moya; Ekurhuleni candidate Xolani Khumalo)

- **Believes:** market-friendly economics, anti-corruption, the toughest line on illegal immigration of any large party, law and order, broadly socially conservative. **On beliefs it is the DA's closest large neighbour.**
- **Needs:** at least one mayoral chain (Joburg for Mashaba, keeping Tshwane for Moya); visible "enforcement" portfolios (metro police, by-law enforcement, immigration-related policing, housing); proof it isn't a DA satellite.
- **Constraints:** it competes with the DA for the same voters in suburbs and in parts of the townships. Junior partners get punished: the UK's Liberal Democrats went from 57 seats to 8 after five years as Conservative junior partners. The 2024 Tshwane rupture left mutual distrust.
- **Says:** no talks before results; the DA must "remove Helen Zille".
- **Will probably do:** run an auction. Settle with the DA if the DA is clearly bigger and the ANC can't make a real offer. Hold out for the chain if the two are close in size or the ANC offers one. "Remove Zille" is an opening bid for the Joburg chain; the real minimum is likely a top post plus enforcement portfolios.
- **Walk-away point:** being junior everywhere with no chain anywhere.

### Economic Freedom Fighters (Julius Malema; Gauteng finance MEC Nkululeko Dunga)

- **Believes:** radical left (nationalisation, expropriation without compensation with state custodianship of land, bringing outsourced municipal work in-house), pan-Africanist and pro-migrant. **It is the opposite of ActionSA on immigration and of the DA on almost everything.**
- **Needs:** relevance after a poor result; its foothold in the provincial executive; keeping the DA from running Gauteng.
- **Constraints:** with roughly 6–8% it is no longer decisive in most council scenarios.
- **Will probably do:** support any ANC-led or ActionSA-led arrangement that keeps the DA out (as it backed Moya in 2024). Its 2016/2021 habit of voting DA mayors in to punish the ANC is unlikely to return, because its enemy ranking has flipped: the DA is now the dominant force, and the ANC is a weakened partner that depends on the EFF. If it is offered nothing anywhere, it will use its provincial seats as leverage, and use negative majorities in councils to remove mayors it dislikes.

### uMkhonto weSizwe (MK)

- **Believes:** "radical economic transformation", parliamentary sovereignty over the Constitution, traditional leadership, a nativist line on immigration; loyal to Zuma, hostile to Ramaphosa.
- **Position in Gauteng:** weak (around 4–5% in the early count), stronger in parts of Ekurhuleni.
- **Will probably do:** sell its votes to negative majorities and stay relevant by embarrassing the ANC. An ANC–MK council deal would be toxic for the GNU; a DA–MK deal is ruled out by beliefs (the Constitution).

### The small parties

| Group | Parties | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| **DA-side regulars** | FF+, ACDP, BOSA, COPE | Close to the DA on beliefs. Will complete DA-led majorities for a portfolio or two. FF+ is especially important in Tshwane |
| **Transactional swing parties** | PA, IFP, Al Jama-ah | Go where the portfolios are. The PA has feuds with the DA but needs to be in government. The IFP leans to GNU-shaped deals. Al Jama-ah held the Joburg chain in 2023 with 3 seats |
| **ANC-side** | PAC, ATM, AIC, UDM | Complete ANC-led blocs |
| **Rise Mzansi** | | Inside Lesufi's provincial coalition but constitutionalist in outlook; could go either way |

**A national lever hidden here:** most of these small parties are members of the national unity government (IFP, PA, FF+, UDM, PAC, Rise Mzansi, Al Jama-ah and GOOD all signed on in 2024). If the ANC and DA agree nationally on how Gauteng should look, they can lean on the very parties that decide knife-edge council majorities.

## 6. Who can work with whom: a map based on beliefs, not statements

| Pairing | Category | Why |
|---|---|---|
| DA + ActionSA | **Natural, at a price** | Closest large pair on economics, institutions, corruption and immigration. Obstacles are personal (Zille–Mashaba), competitive (same voters) and historical (Tshwane 2024), not ideological |
| DA + FF+, ACDP, BOSA, COPE | **Natural** | Long-standing allies; small enough to be cheap |
| ANC + EFF | **Natural on policy, unequal in power** | Overlap on redress and the state's role; three years of co-governing metros and the province. The EFF broke away from the ANC, but that wound has largely healed in practice |
| ANC + PA, IFP, Al Jama-ah, UDM, PAC, ATM, AIC | **Natural (transactional)** | Office-seeking partners in ANC-led councils since 2022 |
| ANC + ActionSA | **Workable at a price** | Far apart on immigration and on ActionSA's anti-corruption brand, but they've co-governed Tshwane since 2024. Works when ActionSA gets the chain |
| DA + PA / DA + IFP | **Workable at a price** | Moderate belief gap; PA–DA personal feuds |
| ANC + DA | **Only under pressure** | A real belief gap on redress and the state's role, and each is the other's main electoral rival. Becomes possible when national leadership frames it as stability or GNU duty |
| ANC + MK | **Only under pressure, and toxic** | Close on policy but existential enemies: MK exists to replace the ANC, and Zuma vs Ramaphosa is personal. Would endanger the GNU |
| ActionSA + EFF in one government | **Only in "anyone but X" mode** | Opposite on immigration. Happened in Tshwane 2024 to keep the DA out; unstable |
| DA + EFF, DA + MK, FF+ + EFF/MK inside government | **Near-impossible** | Fundamental belief gaps (constitution, property, race). Outside votes in a secret ballot remain possible: that is how DA mayors were elected in 2016 and 2021 |

**What the map shows:** two belief blocs. A **market/constitutional bloc** (DA, ActionSA, FF+, ACDP, BOSA, COPE, with the IFP and PA on its edge) and a **redress/statist bloc** (ANC, EFF, MK, PAC, ATM, AIC). **ActionSA belongs to the first bloc on beliefs but is available to the second on organisation.** That is why it is the hinge of the whole province.

## 7. The threshold ladder: what each result would mean

Each rung is a possible result in a metro, from the most DA-favourable to the least. Under each: what follows, why, and what could derail it.

### Rung A: the DA wins an outright majority on its own
- **What follows:** a Cape Town-style single-party government. No coalition politics for five years.
- **Why it's unlikely:** it would need about half the PR vote; even the DA-flattering early count is around 40%.
- **What it would mean:** a decisive blow to the ANC in Gauteng, and the DA's 2029 showcase. The province would treat that metro as the opposition's flagship and fight it over interventions and grants (rung-independent "cohabitation", section 10).

### Rung B: the DA has around 40% or more of the seats
- **What follows:** the DA is indispensable. Nobody can govern without it except a six-or-seven-party anti-DA alliance that would need the ANC, ActionSA, EFF and MK all to cooperate, which beliefs make almost impossible.
- **Partners:** usually ActionSA; possibly only small parties if ActionSA overprices itself.
- **Game-theory lens:** the DA's outside option (a minority government, or small-party deals) is strong, so ActionSA's price falls. ActionSA's best move is to take strong portfolios rather than demand the chain.
- **Derailers:** a Gauteng ANC offering ActionSA the chain *plus* the EFF and MK all agreeing to back it. That's an "anyone but the DA" alliance spanning the immigration divide, possible only for a short time.
- **Precedent:** Sweden 2022. The Moderates formed a government with smaller partners while the Sweden Democrats (larger than the Moderates on the right) supported it from outside through the Tidö agreement. Being central and hard to replace matters more than being the biggest.

### Rung C: the DA and ActionSA together cross 50%+1. Will they form a coalition?

**Short answer: probably yes, but how depends almost entirely on how big each is relative to the other, and on whether the ANC can make ActionSA a rival offer.** This is the most important rung in Gauteng.

**C1. DA much bigger (roughly twice ActionSA or more).** *Likely in Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni if the final count resembles the early one.*
- **Deal:** DA mayor; ActionSA gets major portfolios (public safety, metro police, by-law enforcement, possibly housing or development planning) and perhaps the speaker's chair.
- **Why ActionSA accepts:** it has no better offer. The ANC plus EFF plus MK plus small parties can't reach a majority without the DA. Staying out leaves ActionSA powerless for five years.
- **Game-theory lens:** ActionSA's outside option is weak, so its price is portfolios, not the chain. *Precedent: Germany 2021, where the much smaller FDP joined the coalition but extracted the finance ministry.* Expect ActionSA to fight for one "signature" portfolio it can campaign on in 2029.
- **What could derail it:** Zille personally. If Mashaba makes her removal a matter of honour, the DA faces a chicken game. The likely resolution is a face-saving formula (Zille as mayor with ActionSA controlling enforcement, or a different DA mayor) rather than a breakdown.

**C2. DA moderately bigger (between equal and about twice ActionSA).** *Plausible in Johannesburg if ActionSA does well.*
- **Deal:** contested. Options include:
  - DA mayor with a large ActionSA share of the mayoral committee and the speakership;
  - a **rotating mayoralty** (DA first, ActionSA later, or the reverse);
  - a **compromise mayor**: a DA figure acceptable to Mashaba instead of Zille, or a non-party figure.
- **Why:** both have real alternatives. The DA has small parties or the ANC; ActionSA has an ANC-backed offer. That makes it a genuine negotiation.
- **Precedents:** Ireland 2020, where Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael, historic rivals, rotated the Taoiseach; the Netherlands 2024, where partners refused to serve under Geert Wilders and a non-party prime minister (Dick Schoof) was chosen.
- **What could derail it:** the 14-day clock and pride. If neither swerves, the council could elect a DA minority mayor by default, and the coalition fight moves into the first budget vote.

**C3. Near-equal, or ActionSA bigger.** *Tshwane, and possibly Johannesburg if ActionSA surges.*
- **Deal:** ActionSA has a strong claim to the chain. The DA chooses between serving under an ActionSA mayor and going into opposition.
- **Why the DA might accept:** it needs Gauteng governing experience before 2029; in Tshwane, keeping Moya in office with DA backing is better than watching an ANC–ActionSA–EFF government continue.
- **Why the DA might refuse:** the risk of sharing blame for failures it doesn't control, and the cost to its brand of being junior to a party it considers populist. **Most likely compromise:** the DA accepts an ActionSA mayor in Tshwane and insists on the chain in Johannesburg. A cross-metro trade is the natural deal (section 8).
- **Game-theory lens:** this is a three-player game (DA, ActionSA, ANC) where each pair could govern without the third, so no deal is safe from being outbid. Expect instability, early motions of no confidence and repeated renegotiation unless an outside actor locks in a deal.
- **Precedents:** New Zealand 2017, where the smaller NZ First chose to put second-placed Labour in power over the largest party; Israel 2021, where Naftali Bennett's 7-seat party took the premiership because both sides needed him.

**C4. The two cross 50% by only one or two seats.**
- **What follows:** a DA–ActionSA deal plus a small "insurance" partner (FF+, ACDP, BOSA, IFP) to protect against defections.
- **Why:** the 2021–24 experience: a council where a single councillor can collapse the government ends with Al Jama-ah holding the chain.
- **What could derail it:** a single defector in the secret ballot; small-party demands.

**Things that could break a DA–ActionSA deal even above 50%:**
- **Zille–Mashaba.** A personal feud dating back to 2019. It can be managed by moving Zille, or by giving ActionSA the parts of government it cares most about.
- **ActionSA's fear of being swallowed.** It may rationally prefer to be a kingmaker in opposition than a junior partner in office, *if* it believes the DA-led government will fail and ActionSA can pick up the pieces in 2029.
- **Distrust over Tshwane 2024.** ActionSA's exit from the DA-led coalition, and the DA's accusations of collusion with the ANC, mean both will demand written guarantees and, possibly, national-level backing.
- **The ANC outbidding.** If the ANC offers ActionSA the chain *and* can deliver the EFF and small parties, the DA must match or lose. Whether the ANC can make that offer depends on its internal fight (section 8).

### Rung D: DA + ActionSA short of 50%, but the broader centre-right group (adding FF+, ACDP, BOSA, COPE, IFP, possibly PA) gets there
- **What follows:** a DA-led coalition of five to eight parties. This is a rerun of the 2021 "multi-party coalition" in Johannesburg, which lasted less than a year.
- **Why it's fragile:** every small partner becomes pivotal and charges accordingly (a mayoral-committee seat each, sometimes more). The ANC can poach any one of them.
- **Who holds the cards:** the PA and IFP, both transactional and both in the national unity government, so national leaders can lean on them.
- **What could save it:** the Coalition Bill passing quickly, with its once-a-year limit on motions of no confidence; or a national understanding that the ANC won't poach.

### Rung E: neither the DA–ActionSA pair nor the wider centre-right group reaches 50%
This is where **national politics decides**. Three branches:

- **E1. Grand coalition, ANC + DA.** If ANC national leadership enforces national-unity logic, the ANC accepts a junior role in a DA-led metro government (or, where the ANC is bigger, the reverse), with small GNU parties as ballast.
  - *Precedent:* Germany 2017–18. The SPD swore off another grand coalition; when the alternative talks collapsed, President Steinmeier and the national establishment pushed it back in.
  - *Weakness:* Gauteng ANC structures may rebel (councillors vote in secret), and both parties' voters dislike it.
- **E2. Anti-DA bloc, ANC + ActionSA + EFF + small parties.** If the Gauteng ANC runs its own talks.
  - Likely with ActionSA as mayor if it's close in size to the ANC; otherwise the ANC wears the chain.
  - *Precedent:* Tshwane 2024 itself, and Portugal 2015, where the second-placed Socialists governed with outside support from the radical left after the largest party's government fell within days.
  - *Weakness:* ActionSA and the EFF are opposites on immigration; "anyone but X" coalitions tend to break once the common enemy is out.
- **E3. Minority DA mayor elected by default.** If the anti-DA parties can't agree on a candidate, the DA's candidate can win the elimination-round secret ballot.
  - The government then lives budget by budget, at risk from a negative majority any time the opposition can agree on removal.
  - *Precedents:* Johannesburg and Tshwane 2016 and 2021; minority governments in Denmark and Canada, which survive when the opposition can't agree on an alternative.

### Rung F: ANC + DA together over 50%. When does the grand coalition actually happen?
The arithmetic is available almost everywhere. Beliefs and voters make it a **fallback, not a first choice.** It becomes the outcome when:
- the DA can't reach a majority with ActionSA and small parties, **and**
- the ANC's national leadership wins its internal fight, **and**
- the DA's federal executive decides governing with the ANC beats a fragile multi-party deal.

Most plausible in Tshwane (the capital, where national government presence and GNU symbolism are strongest) and Ekurhuleni (where ANC structures are more pragmatic). Least plausible in Johannesburg, where Zille's candidacy and the ANC's mayoral record make it hardest to sell.

### Rung G: ANC + EFF + ActionSA together over 50%: the Tshwane 2024 template
- **What follows:** if the DA overplays its hand, or the DA–ActionSA pair falls short, this bloc can govern, with ActionSA's mayor where it is close to the ANC in size.
- **Its bigger significance:** even when this bloc never forms, **its mere possibility is ActionSA's leverage.** Every time it has the numbers, ActionSA can credibly ask the DA for more.
- **Weakness:** ActionSA's hard line on immigration and the EFF's pan-Africanism can't coexist for long. Tshwane 2024–26 held because it was a short, anti-DA term.

### Rung H: an ANC-led "progressive" bloc (ANC, EFF, PA, Al Jama-ah, UDM, PAC, ATM, AIC, IFP, Rise Mzansi), with or without MK, crosses 50%
- **What follows:** the 2022–26 arrangement continues. This is only possible if the final count is far better for the ANC and EFF than the early numbers suggest (the ANC near 30%, the DA below about 28%).
- **The MK dilemma:** if MK's seats are needed, the ANC faces its hardest choice. Close on policy, but MK is an existential enemy, and a deal would infuriate the DA nationally.
  - *Precedent:* Germany, January 2025: Friedrich Merz passed a parliamentary motion with far-right AfD votes and paid a heavy political price.
  - Expect the ANC to try to use MK's votes without a formal deal.

### Rung I: no stable majority is possible
- **What follows:** a carousel. A minority mayor elected on a split vote, a crisis at the first adjustment budget (early 2027), motions of no confidence driven by negative majorities, mayors from tiny parties as compromise figures (Al Jama-ah 2023, COPE's Makwarela in Tshwane 2023), court challenges, and talk of provincial intervention.
- **Most at risk:** Tshwane.
- **Precedents:** Italy 2019, where the same prime minister swapped one coalition partner for its opposite mid-term; Malaysia 2020, where a government fell through defections without an election.

## 8. National government pressure: who can lean on Gauteng, and how

National pressure works through four channels: party machines, the national unity government (GNU) bargain, money, and the law. Game theory's **two-level game** is the key lens: every national actor must sell any metro deal to its own Gauteng structures, and every Gauteng actor's options are limited by what national leaders will tolerate.

### A. Party machines
- **ANC national leadership.** Secretary-general Fikile Mbalula has said only the top seven negotiate and warned regions not to open talks. That is the biggest single lever in Gauteng. Its weakness is enforcement: from 2019 to 2024 Gauteng ANC structures made their own EFF deals, and in 2026 Lesufi built the ANC–EFF provincial arrangement.
  - *Precedent:* Thuringia, Germany, February 2020. A state premier elected with far-right AfD votes was forced to resign within days after Chancellor Merkel called the result "unforgivable". **A national party can veto a regional deal, but it costs the national leader too:** the CDU's own leader resigned within the week.
- **ANC succession.** The December 2027 conference ties metro deals to the leadership race. A Gauteng ANC that hands Johannesburg to the DA strengthens the GNU wing; an ANC–EFF–ActionSA bloc strengthens the Gauteng/Mashatile wing. **Expect each faction to judge metro deals partly by what they do to the 2027 race.**
- **DA federal executive.** Coalition decisions are made nationally, not by the Gauteng caucus. Hill-Lewis's 2029 ambition makes the DA *more* willing to pay ActionSA's price and *less* willing to rely on the EFF.

### B. The GNU bargain
- Both the ANC and DA hold hostages: the DA can make GNU life harder if the ANC builds anti-DA coalitions with the EFF or MK; the ANC can squeeze DA ministries. Both blinked during the 2025 budget standoff. **Neither will blow up the GNU over a metro, but both will trade.**
- **The package deal** is the most consequential national-level possibility. An illustrative version:
  - DA mayors in Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni;
  - an ANC-held, ActionSA-held or rotating chain in Tshwane;
  - ANC seats in the metro mayoral committees;
  - possibly a DA role in the provincial executive in return for the ANC dropping the EFF.
  - *Precedents:* Spain, where regional parties trade national votes for regional concessions; Germany, where state coalitions are routinely shaped by federal coalition logic.
- **GNU small parties as the transmission belt.** The IFP, PA, FF+, UDM, PAC, Rise Mzansi and Al Jama-ah all sit in the GNU and hold pivotal council seats. If the ANC and DA agree nationally, they can steer these parties' council votes. That is the quiet way national pressure would show up in council chambers.

### C. Money
- **Grants and Treasury conditions.** Metro infrastructure and trading-services funding is increasingly tied to performance. A national government that wants stable metros can reward coalitions that function and slow-walk money to chaotic ones.
- **The Presidency's direct involvement in Johannesburg** (the 2025 presidential working group on Joburg's infrastructure crisis) gives Ramaphosa a channel to whoever runs the city. That is an incentive to cooperate with national priorities whatever the coalition.

### D. The law
- **Provincial intervention under section 139.** The *province*, not national government, can intervene in a failing municipality, up to dissolving the council. In March 2020 the ANC-run Gauteng executive dissolved the DA-led Tshwane council; the courts later set that aside. **If DA-led coalitions take the metros while the province stays ANC–EFF, expect interventions and grant flows to become political weapons**, restrained mainly by the courts. Under an ANC–DA package deal, the weapon is holstered.
- **The Coalition Bill** (driven by COGTA, under the IFP's Velenkosini Hlabisa) resumes after the election. If it passes with the once-a-year limit on motions of no confidence, the first deal gets locked in. That gives every party a reason to hold out for a good first deal now, and gives small parties maximum leverage *before* it passes.

### How each national stance changes the Gauteng outcome

| National stance | What it looks like | Effect on scenarios |
|---|---|---|
| **Luthuli House discipline** (GNU logic wins) | Top seven negotiate; Lesufi constrained; Ramaphosa and Hill-Lewis talk directly about Gauteng | ActionSA loses its ANC-side option, so its price falls and DA–ActionSA forms more easily. Where DA–ActionSA falls short, the fallback is an ANC–DA grand coalition. The EFF is isolated and turns to the province for leverage |
| **Contested** | Top seven claim control; regions bargain anyway; mixed signals | ActionSA can auction itself; deals take the full 14 days; most risk of default minority mayors and early motions of no confidence |
| **Gauteng autonomy** (Lesufi/Mashatile line wins) | Gauteng ANC offers ActionSA chains and extends the ANC–EFF template | ActionSA's price peaks: the DA concedes chains (Tshwane certainly, maybe Joburg) or loses them to anti-DA blocs. Highest GNU tension. An ANC–MK flirtation in Ekurhuleni becomes thinkable |

## 9. Metro scenario books

Each scenario gives the **trigger** (the result and the political conditions), the **deal**, **why it happens**, **how it breaks**, and **what to watch**.

### Johannesburg (270 seats; 136 needed)

**J1. "Blue–orange Joburg": DA + ActionSA (+ a small partner), DA mayor**
- *Trigger:* DA in the mid-30s or higher, ActionSA mid-teens; together at or just above 136, or just below it with FF+, ACDP or BOSA making up the gap.
- *Deal:* DA mayor; ActionSA gets public safety and enforcement portfolios, probably the speakership; a small insurance partner.
- *Why:* DA and ActionSA are the closest on beliefs; ActionSA has no better offer if the ANC is boxed in nationally.
- *How it breaks:* Zille–Mashaba escalates; ActionSA concludes that being junior will destroy it; a defection in the secret ballot.
- *Watch:* whether Mashaba softens "remove Zille" into a portfolio demand within the first week.
- *Likelihood:* **the most likely single outcome.**

**J2. "Mashaba's price": DA + ActionSA with Mashaba as mayor, or a rotation**
- *Trigger:* ActionSA close to or above 20%, and a credible ANC offer of the chain to Mashaba.
- *Deal:* Mashaba as mayor with a large DA share of the mayoral committee, or a rotation (DA first, Mashaba later, or the reverse).
- *Why:* the DA concludes that losing Joburg to an anti-DA bloc is worse than ceding the chain.
- *How it breaks:* the DA base revolts; Zille refuses to serve under Mashaba; the rotation date becomes a trigger for collapse (as in Maharashtra in 2019, where a dispute over sharing the chief minister's post broke a natural alliance).
- *Likelihood:* **plausible if ActionSA does well.**

**J3. "Zille steps aside"**
- *Trigger:* DA well ahead, but ActionSA makes Zille a deal-breaker and the DA's federal executive wants Joburg more than it wants Zille.
- *Deal:* a different DA mayor; Zille in a senior party role.
- *Why:* the cheapest way for the DA to meet ActionSA's face-saving demand without giving up the chain.
- *How it breaks:* Zille refuses, or the DA reads the demand as blackmail and calls ActionSA's bluff.
- *Likelihood:* **an outside chance, but a classic way out of a chicken game.**

**J4. "Grand bargain Joburg": ANC + DA**
- *Trigger:* DA + ActionSA short of 136, or ActionSA overprices itself, **and** Luthuli House discipline.
- *Deal:* DA mayor, ANC holding several portfolios; part of a national package.
- *Why:* both national leaderships want a stable economic hub; the GNU logic extended.
- *How it breaks:* Gauteng ANC councillors defect in the secret ballot; DA voters revolt; the ANC's 2027 race turns on it.
- *Likelihood:* **plausible as a fallback, unlikely as a first choice.**

**J5. "Anti-DA rainbow": ANC + ActionSA + EFF + small parties**
- *Trigger:* DA in the low 30s or below, DA + ActionSA short, **and** Gauteng autonomy.
- *Deal:* Mashaba as mayor if ActionSA is close to the ANC in size; otherwise an ANC mayor with ActionSA and EFF portfolios.
- *Why:* the ANC returns to government; ActionSA gets the chain; the EFF keeps the DA out.
- *How it breaks:* immigration and policing policy (ActionSA's core) clashes with the EFF's; the GNU reacts; national leadership disciplines Gauteng (Thuringia-style).
- *Likelihood:* **plausible if the count shifts toward the polls.**

**J6. "Default minority mayor"**
- *Trigger:* no deal by the first sitting; anti-DA parties can't unite behind one candidate.
- *Deal:* the DA candidate wins the elimination-round secret ballot without a coalition.
- *Why:* the rules let a plurality survive if the opposition is divided.
- *How it breaks:* the first budget; a negative majority (ANC + ActionSA + EFF + MK) removes the mayor once it can agree on a replacement.
- *Likelihood:* **possible if talks run out the clock.**

**J7. "The carousel": repeated collapses**
- *Trigger:* thin majorities, Zille–Mashaba unresolved, ANC autonomy, no Coalition Bill.
- *What happens:* 2022–24 repeated: several mayors in two years, a tiny-party compromise mayor at some point, a frozen administration.
- *Likelihood:* **a significant risk in every scenario except J1/J3 with a buffer and J4.**

**J8. "Compromise mayor": neither the DA nor ActionSA wears the chain**
- *Trigger:* DA and ActionSA close in size and neither will concede.
- *Deal:* a small-party or non-party mayor (Schoof-style) with a DA–ActionSA mayoral committee.
- *Why:* the Netherlands 2024 solution to "we'll govern with you but not under you".
- *How it breaks:* the mayor has no base; the larger party governs from behind and resents the arrangement.
- *Likelihood:* **an outside chance.**

**J9. "ANC chooses opposition"**
- *Trigger:* the ANC around 20%, offered only junior roles; leadership decides renewal before 2027/2029 is worth more than mayoral-committee seats.
- *What happens:* the DA + ActionSA (+ smalls) govern and own Joburg's infrastructure crisis; the ANC campaigns against them with the province's backing.
- *Why it's rational:* incumbents in a broken city get blamed. *Precedent:* the SPD's 2017 instinct to rebuild in opposition (later overridden by national pressure).
- *Likelihood:* **unlikely: it runs against the ANC's office-seeking culture, but it is a coherent strategy.**

**J10. "National co-governance"**
- *Trigger:* any fragile outcome (J6, J7).
- *What happens:* the Presidency's Joburg working group, Treasury conditions and the threat of provincial intervention mean effective power shifts upward. National government sets terms on water, power and finance whoever holds the chain.
- *Likelihood:* **partly already true; grows with every collapse.**

### Tshwane (214 seats; 108 needed): the least stable metro

**T1. "Moya stays with DA backing": ActionSA mayor, DA + ActionSA + FF+**
- *Trigger:* ActionSA finishes level with or ahead of the DA (as the early count suggests).
- *Deal:* Nasiphi Moya remains mayor; the DA takes the speakership and major portfolios; FF+ is the third partner.
- *Why:* ActionSA has both the bigger bloc and the ANC-side alternative; the DA would rather back Moya than watch an ANC–ActionSA–EFF coalition continue.
- *How it breaks:* the DA–ActionSA grudge from 2024; DA brand worries; the FF+ resisting an ActionSA-led government if it leans populist.
- *Likelihood:* **the leading scenario if ActionSA holds its early lead.**

**T2. "Brink returns": DA mayor (Cilliers Brink) with ActionSA + FF+**
- *Trigger:* the DA recovers in late counting and finishes clearly ahead of ActionSA.
- *Deal:* Brink as mayor, with a big ActionSA share of the mayoral committee.
- *Why:* the DA's claim as the larger party; the ANC boxed in nationally.
- *How it breaks:* ActionSA, which helped remove Brink in 2024, may refuse to restore him; a face-saving compromise (a different DA mayor) may be needed.
- *Likelihood:* **plausible if the DA finishes clearly first.**

**T3. "The Moya coalition continues": ANC + ActionSA + EFF + small parties**
- *Trigger:* DA + ActionSA short, or the DA refuses to serve under Moya; Gauteng autonomy.
- *Deal:* Moya stays mayor on ANC and EFF votes, as now.
- *Why:* continuity; the ANC stays in government; the EFF keeps the DA out.
- *How it breaks:* GNU pressure; contradictions between ActionSA and the EFF; the DA campaigning to split ActionSA from the ANC.
- *Likelihood:* **plausible.**

**T4. "Capital-city grand bargain": ANC + DA**
- *Trigger:* Tshwane three-way deadlock **and** Luthuli House discipline.
- *Deal:* a DA or ANC mayor (whichever is larger), the other with major portfolios; possibly a rotation.
- *Why:* Pretoria is the seat of national government; the national unity government's symbolism is strongest here; it removes ActionSA's leverage.
- *How it breaks:* local ANC and DA structures in Tshwane have the bitterest relationship of the three metros.
- *Likelihood:* **an outside chance, rising if the three-way deadlock lasts.**

**T5. "ANC first, ActionSA junior"**
- *Trigger:* the ANC finishes first.
- *Deal:* an ANC mayor with ActionSA and the EFF.
- *Why:* the largest-party claim, plus ActionSA's preference for any government over opposition.
- *How it breaks:* ActionSA demands the chain it already holds.
- *Likelihood:* **less likely; ActionSA won't readily give up a chain it has.**

**T6. "Three-way stalemate"**
- *Trigger:* DA, ActionSA and ANC all within a few points and no two will agree.
- *What happens:* a default minority mayor, a budget crisis, motions of no confidence, possibly a renewed **provincial intervention** (the 2020 dissolution precedent), which the courts would test.
- *Likelihood:* **a real risk in Tshwane specifically.**

**T7. "Rotating mayoralty": DA and ActionSA split the term**
- *Trigger:* DA and ActionSA nearly equal; neither will serve under the other.
- *Why:* the Irish 2020 device.
- *How it breaks:* the handover date becomes a cliff; the outgoing party may defect before handing over.
- *Likelihood:* **possible.**

### Ekurhuleni (about 224 seats; about 113 needed)

**E1. "Blue–orange Ekurhuleni": DA + ActionSA (+ a small partner), DA mayor**
- *Trigger:* DA in the mid-30s or higher, ActionSA mid-teens.
- *Deal:* DA mayor; Xolani Khumalo's ActionSA gets enforcement portfolios.
- *Why:* the same logic as J1; ActionSA's high-profile Ekurhuleni candidate is anti-crime-branded, so a public safety portfolio is a natural fit.
- *How it breaks:* thin majority; ANC poaching of small partners.
- *Likelihood:* **the most likely outcome.**

**E2. "ActionSA's price in Ekurhuleni"**
- *Trigger:* ActionSA close to the DA.
- *Deal:* a senior ActionSA role, possibly the chain, traded against the DA holding Johannesburg (section 10).
- *Likelihood:* **possible as part of a cross-metro trade.**

**E3. "Grand bargain Ekurhuleni": ANC + DA**
- *Trigger:* DA + ActionSA short and national discipline.
- *Why:* the Ekurhuleni ANC is historically more pragmatic than Johannesburg's.
- *Likelihood:* **plausible as a fallback.**

**E4. "Anti-DA bloc": ANC + ActionSA + EFF (+ small parties)**
- *Trigger:* DA + ActionSA short and Gauteng autonomy.
- *Likelihood:* **plausible if the count moves toward the polls.**

**E5. "The MK surprise": ANC + EFF + MK**
- *Trigger:* MK outperforms its early count (mid-2026 polling had it far higher in Ekurhuleni).
- *Why:* closest beliefs on the redress side; the ANC's hardest choice.
- *How it breaks:* GNU partners' fury; ANC national leadership intervenes; the arrangement may operate only through votes without a formal deal.
- *Likelihood:* **fading on the early count, but the scenario with the biggest national shock if it happens.**

**E6. "Default minority mayor" / E7. "Carousel"**: as in Johannesburg.

### Other Gauteng councils
The same logic, smaller stakes:
- **Midvaal:** the DA's to lose.
- **Emfuleni:** financially distressed and with a history of provincial intervention. An ANC collapse here opens the council to a DA-led or multi-party government that would inherit a fiscal crisis.
- **Mogale City, Rand West, Merafong, Lesedi:** where the ANC's fall makes it a junior bidder for the first time, and where the PA, ActionSA and local parties will be pivotal.

Expect deals in these councils to be traded as part of metro negotiations ("we back you in Mogale, you back us in Emfuleni").

## 10. Gauteng-wide end-states: how metro outcomes combine with the province

Single-metro scenarios combine into a handful of Gauteng-wide pictures, each with a provincial consequence.

### G1. "Cohabitation": DA-led Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni (with ActionSA), contested Tshwane, ANC–EFF province — *most likely*
- The ANC and EFF become joint opposition in the councils and cling together in the legislature.
- **Lesufi survives:** without the EFF, the provincial opposition (DA, MK, ActionSA, FF+ and smaller parties) can't reach the 41 seats needed to remove him.
- **What follows:** an open contest between DA-run cities and an ANC–EFF province over interventions, provincial grants, policing (metro police vs provincial police), housing and transport. Every service failure becomes a blame game.
- **Precedents:** France's "cohabitation" periods (president and prime minister from rival camps); South Africa's own 2009–2024 Western Cape, a DA province governing under an ANC national government, which shows this can be stable, just adversarial.
- **The 2029 dress rehearsal:** the DA wants to show it can fix Johannesburg; the ANC–EFF province wants to show it can't.

### G2. "Grand bargain Gauteng": ANC–DA in the metros and the province
- **Trigger:** Luthuli House discipline plus a national package; DA + ActionSA short in at least one metro, or ActionSA overpricing itself.
- **Province:** the EFF is dropped from the executive and the DA joins. ANC + DA hold about 50 of 80 seats, the most stable provincial government possible.
- **Losers:** the EFF (out everywhere), ActionSA (leverage gone), the Gauteng ANC faction (humiliated before 2027).
- **Risks:** a revolt by ANC Gauteng structures; secret-ballot defections; DA voter backlash. *Precedent:* Germany 2018. The grand coalition worked as government but bled support from both partners.

### G3. "Split Gauteng": DA-led Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni; ANC–ActionSA–EFF Tshwane
- **Trigger:** clear DA leads in two metros; three-way deadlock in Tshwane resolved on the ANC side.
- **Province:** stays ANC–EFF; Tshwane becomes the ANC's showcase, and the capital the main arena for ANC–DA rivalry.
- **Logic:** a natural cross-metro trade. ActionSA gets Tshwane (Moya), the DA gets the other two. The trade could be made on either side of the aisle.

### G4. "ActionSA's Gauteng": ActionSA mayors in Tshwane (and possibly Johannesburg) with DA backing
- **Trigger:** ActionSA strong, Gauteng ANC credible as an alternative partner, the DA wanting government more than the chain.
- **Consequence:** the DA is the biggest party in Gauteng's councils but wears no chain in its biggest city. A brand problem for the DA before 2029, a breakthrough for ActionSA.
- **Risk:** the DA tires of carrying ActionSA mayors and withdraws, triggering carousels.

### G5. "Red-green Gauteng": ANC-led blocs hold the metros, with the ANC–EFF province
- **Trigger:** only if the final count moves sharply toward the pre-election polls (ANC near 30%, DA below 30%).
- **Consequence:** continuity of the 2022–26 arrangement; an ANC–EFF(–MK) "progressive" axis consolidates, strengthening Mashatile's wing before 2027; GNU tension rises sharply.

### G6. "Carousel Gauteng": no stable deals anywhere
- **Trigger:** thin majorities, ANC infighting, ActionSA auctions, no Coalition Bill.
- **Consequence:** repeated motions of no confidence, minority mayors, provincial intervention threats, possibly fast-tracking of the Coalition Bill as an emergency fix, and growing national co-governance of Johannesburg.

## 11. The provincial government: end-states and the chains that lead to them

**Legislature (80 seats, 41 needed):** ANC 28, DA 22, EFF 11, MK 8, ActionSA 3, FF+ 2, PA 2, IFP 1, ACDP 1, Rise Mzansi 1, BOSA 1. **Lesufi's working majority:** ANC + EFF + IFP + PA + Rise Mzansi, about 43 seats.

| Provincial end-state | Causal chain | Likelihood |
|---|---|---|
| **P1. ANC–EFF executive survives to 2029** | DA-led metros push the ANC and EFF into joint opposition; the EFF's only remaining leverage is provincial, so it stays in and extracts more (the finance portfolio is already its) | **Most likely** |
| **P2. ANC–DA provincial deal** (EFF out, DA in) | A national package (G2). The DA gets provincial portfolios; the ANC gets metro portfolios and stability | Plausible but needs national discipline to win decisively |
| **P3. ANC limps on as a minority (about 32 seats) without the EFF** | The ANC partners the DA in a metro against EFF wishes. The EFF withdraws its MEC and votes against budgets. The ANC governs bill by bill, leaning on MK or the DA case by case | Possible, especially if G2 is only partly delivered |
| **P4. Lesufi replaced by his own party** | The ANC's worst Gauteng result ever; national leadership blames provincial leadership; redeployment before 2027 | Possible; depends on how the ANC's internal blame game goes |
| **P5. A formal ANC–EFF–MK "progressive" provincial bloc** | Gauteng autonomy wins and the ANC leads at least one metro on EFF/MK votes; Lesufi completes the talks with MK he has been running | Unlikely; would provoke a national GNU crisis |

**The coupling to remember:** every metro the ANC governs with the DA makes P3 more likely; every metro where the ANC is pushed into opposition makes P1 more likely. **The EFF's leverage shifts from councils to the legislature.** Its 11 seats are what keep Lesufi in office.

## 12. How scenarios evolve over time

1. **Declaration to first sitting (8 to about 22 November).** Public positioning gives way to private bargaining. Stated red lines matter most here, because no one wants to be seen backing down in public. Expect brinkmanship to the last day and, possibly, default minority mayors where talks fail.
2. **The first 100 days.** Mayoral committees are allocated; written coalition agreements are tested; small parties test their leverage. Weak deals show cracks.
3. **First adjustment budget (early 2027) and the 2027/28 budget (by mid-2027).** The real test: a majority of all councillors is needed. Minority mayors fall or cut deals here.
4. **The Coalition Bill.** If passed with the once-a-year limit on motions of no confidence, whatever exists at that point gets locked in. Expect parties to time manoeuvres around it.
5. **ANC national conference (December 2027).** Metro arrangements will be judged by what they do for each faction. A change of ANC leadership could flip national posture from GNU discipline to Gauteng autonomy or the reverse, and with it every grand-coalition deal.
6. **Toward 2029.** The DA needs visible results in whatever it governs; the ANC–EFF province needs to show DA cities failing; ActionSA needs to show it isn't a DA satellite. All three incentives push toward conflict in year three.

**The 2021 lesson:** in 2021 all three metros elected DA mayors, on the back of public positions (ActionSA's anti-ANC pledge, the EFF's tactic of punishing the ANC). Within 12 to 36 months, Ekurhuleni had moved to the ANC and EFF, and Tshwane to ANC + ActionSA + EFF. **Words decided the first vote; beliefs and numbers decided what lasted.** Read the November 2026 mayoral votes the same way.

## 13. International precedents, by mechanism

| What could happen in Gauteng | Precedent | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Ideological neighbours fall out over the top job (DA vs ActionSA) | **Maharashtra, India, 2019**: BJP and Shiv Sena won together, split over who would be chief minister; the Sena governed with former opponents, and fell in 2022 through a revolt | A natural alliance can fail over office alone; the replacement coalition is weaker |
| Giving the top job to a smaller partner or a neutral | **Ireland 2020** (rotating Taoiseach between historic rivals); **Netherlands 2024** (non-party prime minister instead of Wilders); **Israel 2021** (a 7-seat party's leader as prime minister) | Rotation and compromise leaders unlock deals; "kingmaker premierships" are fragile (Israel's lasted a year; the Dutch coalition collapsed in 2025) |
| Junior partner extracts a signature portfolio | **Germany 2021** (FDP took the finance ministry) | ActionSA's realistic price in a DA-dominated council is a defining portfolio, not the chain |
| Second-placed party governs | **New Zealand 2017** (NZ First chose Labour over the largest party); **Portugal 2015** (Socialists governed with radical-left support after the largest party's government fell) | Being largest guarantees nothing; the pivotal party chooses |
| Outside support instead of coalition | **Sweden 2022** (Tidö agreement); **Canada 2022–24** (Liberal–NDP supply and confidence); **Denmark** (minority governments are the norm) | Works while the supporter hates the alternative more; ends when that calculation flips, as the EFF's did in 2022 |
| National leadership overrides a regional deal | **Thuringia, Germany, 2020** (Merkel forced a premier elected with AfD votes to resign) | National parties can veto regional deals, quickly, but at a cost to their own leader |

| Red lines dissolve as alternatives fail | **Austria 2024–25** (ÖVP refused the far-right leader, then negotiated with him, then returned to a centrist three-party deal) | Watch the second and third rounds of talks, not the first-day statements |
| Using pariah votes without a deal | **Germany, January 2025** (a CDU motion passed with AfD votes, causing a national backlash) | Accepting EFF or MK votes in a secret ballot is cheaper than a deal, but not free |
| Junior-partner punishment | **United Kingdom 2010–15** (Liberal Democrats fell from 57 seats to 8) | ActionSA and the ANC both fear being junior to the DA, and will demand visible wins |
| Mid-term partner swaps | **Italy 2019** (the same prime minister swapped one partner for its opposite); **Malaysia 2020** (government fell through defections) | South Africa's negative majorities do the same in councils; the Coalition Bill would slow them |
| Rival-camp governments at two levels | **France's cohabitation** periods; **Western Cape 2009–24** under an ANC national government | DA metros under an ANC–EFF province can be stable but adversarial; expect fights over powers and money |
| Very long formations | **Belgium 2010–11** (541 days) | South Africa's 14-day clock prevents this, but forces half-built deals that unravel later |
| Regional parties trading national support for regional concessions | **Spain 2023** (Junts' 7 seats decided the national government in exchange for concessions) | GNU small parties (IFP, PA, FF+, UDM, PAC, Al Jama-ah) can trade council votes for national favours, and be leaned on the same way |

## 14. Signposts and the decision tree

**Watch, in order:**
1. **Final seat totals against the lines** (from 8 November): DA + ActionSA against 136 in Johannesburg, 108 in Tshwane and 113 in Ekurhuleni.
2. **The DA-to-ActionSA size ratio** in each metro. Close to equal means ActionSA bids for the chain.
3. **Who speaks for the ANC:** Mbalula and the top seven, or Lesufi and the Gauteng leadership. Whether ANC mayoral candidates are named, and by whom.
4. **Mashaba's language in week one:** "remove Zille" softening into portfolio demands means J1/J3; hardening means J2/J5.
5. **The EFF's provincial posture:** any threat to the finance portfolio or the adjustment budget means the EFF is using the province to influence the metro talks.
6. **A Ramaphosa–Hill-Lewis meeting about Gauteng:** a package deal (G2) becomes live and ActionSA's price drops.
7. **Small GNU parties' behaviour** (PA, IFP, FF+, Al Jama-ah): moving together signals national coordination.
8. **First council sittings:** secret-ballot surprises.
9. **The Coalition Bill timetable.**

**Decision tree:**

```
Does DA + ActionSA cross 50%+1 in the metro?
├─ YES
│  ├─ DA much bigger than ActionSA → J1 / E1 (DA mayor, ActionSA portfolios); Zille question resolved by face-saving (J3)
│  ├─ DA somewhat bigger → price fight: DA mayor + big ActionSA share, rotation, or compromise mayor (J2, J8, T7)
│  └─ Near-equal / ActionSA bigger → ActionSA claims the chain (T1, G4); DA accepts in Tshwane, resists in Joburg
│     └─ If the ANC can credibly offer ActionSA the chain → ActionSA's price rises; risk of carousel (J7, T6)
└─ NO
   ├─ Wider centre-right group (with FF+, ACDP, IFP, PA…) over 50%? → fragile 5–8 party DA-led coalition (Rung D)
   └─ Still short → who controls the ANC?
      ├─ Luthuli House / GNU discipline → ANC–DA grand coalition (J4, T4, E3; G2 province deal possible)
      ├─ Gauteng autonomy → anti-DA bloc: ANC + ActionSA + EFF (+MK in Ekurhuleni) (J5, T3, E4, E5)
      └─ Nobody controls it → default minority mayor, budget crisis, carousel (J6, J7, T6)

Then, province: ANC with DA in metros → EFF strain (P2 or P3); ANC in opposition in metros → ANC–EFF bond tightens (P1)
```
