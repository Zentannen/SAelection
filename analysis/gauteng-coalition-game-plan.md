# Coalition scenarios by vote share and values: lessons from Eastern Europe and beyond

*A scenario playbook for hung councils, built from what has happened in other countries. South Africa appears only at the end, where the patterns are mapped onto Gauteng's three metros and its province. No 2026 news, polls or party statements are used; the only South African input needed is the official result, expressed as vote shares.*

---

## How to read this

- **Percentages** are vote shares (and, in proportional systems, roughly seat shares). A configuration is described by the shares of the main parties, for example "largest party 30–35%, runner-up 25–30%, challenger 10–15%".
- **Values** describe where parties stand, in archetypes rather than names:
  - economic (state ↔ market);
  - identity (nationalist or redistributive-identity ↔ liberal-universalist);
  - institutional (patronage, capture ↔ rule of law, anti-corruption);
  - migration (open ↔ hardline).
- **Each configuration** comes with real cases: country, year, percentages, the government that formed, how long it lasted, and why.
- **Game theory is used as a tool, not a proof.** Concepts like the pivotal party, the outside option and the credible threat explain *why* cases went the way they did. The cases themselves are the evidence.

Most cases are from **Central and Eastern Europe**. Since 1990 that region has produced the widest range of fragmented parliaments, declining dominant parties, anti-corruption newcomers, personalist splinters and cross-ideological "anyone but X" coalitions. Western European, Israeli, Indian and Antipodean cases fill gaps.

## 1. The archetypes

| Archetype | Values profile | Foreign examples | Gauteng counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Declining dominant party** | Broad church, redistributive, strong patronage networks, office-seeking | PSD (Romania), Smer (Slovakia), GERB (Bulgaria), PDM (Moldova), SLD (Poland) | ANC |
| **Liberal-reformist party** | Market economics, rule of law, anti-corruption, institution-minded | PO/KO (Poland), ODS and SPOLU (Czechia), PNL and USR (Romania), PS and SaS (Slovakia), Reform (Estonia), DB (Bulgaria) | DA |
| **Anti-corruption challenger** | Founded by a businessman or outsider; anti-establishment, centrist-to-right on economics, hard on crime and often migration; personalist | ANO (Czechia), OĽaNO (Slovakia), Most (Croatia), PP and ITN (Bulgaria), KPV LV (Latvia), Res Publica (Estonia) | ActionSA |
| **Radical-left flank party** | Further left than the dominant party, often born from a rupture with it; prefers outside influence or toleration to shared responsibility | Levica (Slovenia), KSČM (Czechia), Bloco de Esquerda (Portugal) | EFF |
| **Personalist splinter led by a former leader** | Close to the parent on policy, hostile to its current leadership | Hlas (Pellegrini from Smer, Slovakia); Pro România (Ponta from PSD, Romania) | MK |
| **Community or identity kingmaker** | Represents a defined community; joins whoever governs in return for specific benefits | UDMR (Hungarians in Romania), DPS (Turks in Bulgaria), Most-Híd (Slovakia), Swedish People's Party (Finland), Albanian parties (North Macedonia) | PA, IFP, FF+, Al Jama-ah and similar |

## 2. Values distances between archetypes

| Pair | Distance | What usually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Liberal-reformist + community kingmaker | **Adjacent** | Routine partnerships (Finland's Swedish People's Party; UDMR in Romanian centre-right governments) |
| Liberal-reformist + anti-corruption challenger | **Adjacent on policy, competitive for voters** | Coalitions do form, but the two fight over the top post and over who owns the "clean government" brand (Poland 2005 failed; Czechia 2021 worked; Slovakia 2020 formed then broke) |
| Declining dominant party + its splinters or radical flank | **Adjacent on policy, hostile personally** | Splinters often return to the parent in coalition once the personal conflict is managed (Slovakia 2023) |
| Declining dominant party + community kingmaker | **Adjacent (transactional)** | Routine (UDMR with PSD in Romania; DPS with BSP in Bulgaria) |
| Declining dominant party + anti-corruption challenger | **Bridgeable only if the challenger gets the top job or a clear anti-corruption win** | Rare and short (Bulgaria's GERB with PP-DB, 2023–24, collapsed at the rotation point) |
| Liberal-reformist + declining dominant party | **Historic rivals** | Grand coalitions happen when a third force is excluded or the alternatives have failed (Romania 2021; Germany 2018; Czechia's 1998 toleration pact) |
| Liberal-reformist + radical-left flank | **Far apart** | At most outside support or toleration, almost never a shared government |
| Anti-corruption challenger + radical-left flank | **Far apart (especially on migration)** | Only in "anyone but X" coalitions, which break fast (Moldova 2019; Bulgaria 2021–22) |

## 3. Configurations and what happened elsewhere

Each configuration lists the closest foreign cases, with percentages, outcome, lifespan and the reason.

### Configuration 1: one party at about 40% or more, with a small adjacent partner available

| Case | Percentages | Government | Lifespan | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Poland 2007** | PO 41.5, PiS 32.1, LiD 13.2, PSL 8.9 | PO + PSL (agrarian, community-type partner) | Full term, then renewed in 2011 (PO 39.2, PSL 8.4) | Dominant partner, a small partner with a clear constituency, little overlap in voters |
| **Croatia 2024** | HDZ 34.4, SDP-led bloc 25.4, DP 9.6, Možemo 9.1, Most 8.0 | HDZ + DP (the kingmaker chose the larger party closer to it in values over the "anyone but HDZ" bloc) | Ongoing | The kingmaker's values pointed to the larger party, not to the bigger offer |
| **Slovakia 2012** | Smer 44.4 | Smer alone (wasted votes from small parties gave 44% a majority of seats) | Full term | Fragmented small parties below the threshold inflate the leader's seat share |

**Lesson:** at about 40%, the leading party picks its partner; the outcome is stable, often for a full term. The challenger's best play is portfolios, not the top job.

### Configuration 2: the liberal party and the anti-corruption challenger together exceed 50%. Do they coalesce?

| Case | Percentages | What happened | Lifespan | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Poland 2005** | PiS 27.0, PO 24.1 (together 288 of 460 seats) | The expected "POPiS" coalition of two post-Solidarity neighbours **did not form**. PiS governed as a minority, then with two populist parties | About 15 months, then an early election | Similar sizes, the same voter pool, a fight over the top posts. Each preferred to beat the other |
| **Czechia 2021** | SPOLU 27.8, PirStan 15.6 vs ANO 27.1 | SPOLU + PirStan formed the government; ANO (largest by seats) was excluded | Full term | **Pre-committed before the election**, and SPOLU was clearly the senior partner |
| **Slovakia 2020** | OĽaNO 25.0 (challenger), Sme Rodina 8.2, SaS 6.2 (liberal), Za ľudí 5.8 | Challenger-led four-party government | About 33 months; the liberal partner left, then no-confidence and an early election | The challenger's personalist leader clashed with the liberals |
| **Latvia 2018** | Harmony 19.8 (excluded), KPV LV 14.3 (challenger), JKP 13.6, AP 12.0, NA 11.0, JV 6.7 | Five-party coalition led by the smallest party's leader | Full term | The challenger joined as one of several partners; the compromise PM was trusted by all |
| **Croatia 2016** | HDZ-led 33.4, SDP-led 33.2, Most 13.5 (challenger) | Most backed HDZ with a non-party PM | About 5 months; a second Most–HDZ deal also broke within months | The newcomer challenger kept threatening to walk; the compromise PM had no base |
| **Bulgaria 2021** | PP 25.7 (challenger), BSP 10.2, ITN 9.5, DB 6.4 (liberal) | Four-party anti-corruption coalition | About 6 months | ITN (another challenger) walked out; too many personalist leaders |

**Lesson:** when the liberal party and the challenger together pass 50%, a coalition is **likely but not automatic**. It forms most reliably when:
- one partner is clearly larger (about 1.5–2× or more);
- the two pre-committed before the vote;
- the challenger gets a defining portfolio rather than the top job.

It fails or breaks when they are similar in size and compete for the same voters (Poland 2005), or when the challenger's leader is highly personalist (Slovakia 2020, Croatia 2016).

**Portfolio expectations (Gamson's law).** Across parliamentary democracies, coalition partners receive cabinet posts roughly in proportion to the seats they bring, with small pivotal partners slightly over-rewarded. A challenger bringing a third of the coalition's seats should expect about a third of the executive posts, plus a premium if it is pivotal.

### Configuration 3: three-way split (the top three each between about 15% and 30%, nobody near 40%)

| Case | Percentages | Government | Lifespan | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Slovenia 2018** | SDS 24.9 (excluded), LMŠ 12.6, SD 9.9, SMC 9.8, Levica 9.3 | Second-placed LMŠ, with half the leader's vote, led a five-party minority government, with Levica supporting from outside | About 16 months; Levica withdrew, the PM resigned, and the excluded largest party then formed a government with defectors from the old bloc | Outside support ended when the radical supporter's priorities diverged |
| **Estonia 2019** | Reform 28.9 (excluded), Centre 23.1, EKRE 17.8, Isamaa 11.4 | The second-placed party governed with the radical right | About 21 months | The second-placed party reached for the radical flank; a scandal ended it |
| **Croatia 2015** | 33.4 / 33.2 / 13.5 | Months of talks; a technocrat PM | About 5 months | Two equal blocs and a volatile kingmaker |
| **Bulgaria 2021–24** | Repeated splits of roughly 25 / 23 / 13 / 10 | Seven elections in about 3½ years | Governments of 6–9 months | The anti-corruption camp refused the old dominant party; the old dominant party couldn't find partners |
| **Ireland 2020** | SF 24.5 (most votes, excluded), FF 22.2, FG 20.9, Greens 7.1 | FF + FG + Greens with a rotating Taoiseach | Full term, rotation completed | Historic rivals united against a third force and used rotation to share the top job |

**Lesson:** in three-way splits, **the largest party is often excluded**; second or even fifth place can lead. Governments are short unless the two partners are institutional parties that agree a rotation in writing. Where new elections are available, deadlock leads to repeated elections (Bulgaria). Where they aren't, it leads to repeated reshuffles and changes of leader.

### Configuration 4: the declining dominant party plus its splinters and radical flank reach 50%

| Case | Percentages | Government | Lifespan | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Slovakia 2023** | Smer 22.9, PS 18.0 (liberal, second), Hlas 14.7 (Smer splinter), SNS 5.6 | Smer + Hlas + SNS. The splinter returned to its parent instead of joining the liberals | Ongoing; the splinter's leader later took the presidency | Values pulled the splinter home; it extracted a very high price (speakership, then the presidency) |
| **Slovakia 2006** | Smer 29.1, SNS 11.7, HZDS 8.8 | Smer with nationalist partners | Full term, but the European socialist party family **suspended Smer** for it | A party family above the national level can sanction coalition choices, but the sanction didn't change the outcome |
| **Czechia 2017–18** | ANO 29.6; others refused | First an ANO minority (lost a confidence vote), then ANO + ČSSD with the communists tolerating from outside | Full term (the communists withdrew support near the end) | When the liberal parties refuse, the leading party turns to the radical left's toleration |
| **Romania (PSD and its splinters)** | PSD around 30–45% in different years | Ponta's Pro România and Tăriceanu's ALDE split from or allied with PSD, then largely drifted back | – | Personalist splinters are often temporary |

**Lesson:** splinters usually stay close to the parent on values. **The likeliest outcome is reunion in coalition once personal leadership conflicts are managed or the leadership changes.** The price is high: the splinter's leader gets a top post.

### Configuration 5: neither values bloc reaches 50%, or a radical bloc has to be excluded: the grand coalition of rivals

| Case | Percentages | Government | Lifespan | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Romania 2021** | PSD 28.9, PNL 25.2, USR 15.4, AUR 9.1, UDMR 5.7 | First PNL + USR + UDMR (the values bloc), which broke after about 10 months. Then **PNL + PSD + UDMR, a grand coalition of historic rivals with a rotating PM**, brokered by the president | Full term; the rotation was carried out on schedule in 2023 | Both are institutional parties with patronage interests; a president brokered it; a fixed rotation made the bargain credible |
| **Romania 2024** | PSD 22.0, AUR 18.0, PNL 13.2, USR 12.4, SOS 7.4, POT 6.5, UDMR 6.3 | Pro-EU rivals combined to exclude a radical bloc of about 32% | Ongoing | A cordon around radicals forces rivals together |
| **Czechia 1998** | ČSSD 32.3, ODS 27.7 | The "opposition agreement": the main rival tolerated a minority government in exchange for institutional posts | Full term | Toleration instead of coalition lets rivals avoid sharing blame |
| **Germany 2017–18** | CDU/CSU 32.9, SPD 20.5, AfD 12.6, FDP 10.7 | The SPD had ruled out a grand coalition; after other talks collapsed, the president pressed it, and it formed one | Full term | Pressure from the head of state; the alternatives had visibly failed |
| **Bulgaria 2023** | GERB 26.5, PP-DB 24.6 | A government of rivals (the old dominant party and the anti-corruption challenger) with a planned rotation | About 9 months; **collapsed at the rotation point** | One partner's identity was built on opposing the other; the handover forced the contradiction into the open |
| **Austria 2024–25** | FPÖ 28.8, ÖVP 26.3, SPÖ 21.1, NEOS 9.1 | A centrist three-party deal failed, the largest-party option failed, then the centrist deal was revived | Ongoing | Red lines dissolved round by round, then reformed |

**Lesson:** grand coalitions of rivals **form when a higher authority pushes and the alternatives have failed**. They last when both partners are institutional parties and the top-job question is settled in writing (Romania 2021). They break when one partner exists to oppose the other (Bulgaria 2023).

### Configuration 6: "anyone but X" coalitions spanning opposite values

| Case | Percentages | Government | Lifespan | Why it ended |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Moldova 2019** | PSRM 31.2 (pro-Russian left), ACUM 26.8 (pro-EU reformers) united against PDM 23.6 | Joint government to remove a dominant patronage party | About 5 months | Once the common enemy was gone, nothing held them together |
| **Slovakia 2010** | Smer 34.8 excluded; SDKÚ 15.4 + SaS 12.1 + KDH 8.5 + Most-Híd 8.1 | Four-party centre-right coalition | About 15 months | One partner broke ranks on a single big vote |
| **Israel 2021** | Likud 24.2 excluded; eight parties from right to Arab Islamist, led by Yamina (6.2%, 7 seats) | "Anyone but Netanyahu" government | About 12 months | Defections from the smallest ideological edges |
| **Karnataka, India, 2018** | BJP 104 seats (largest); INC 80; JD(S) 37 seats (about 18% of the vote) | The third party's leader became chief minister with second-placed Congress's support | About 14 months | Defections engineered by the excluded largest party |
| **Hungary 2022** | Fidesz 54.1 vs a united left-to-right opposition 34.4 | Opposition alliance lost, then dissolved | – | Ideologically mixed alliances underperform with voters as well |

**Lesson:** coalitions held together only by a common enemy last **roughly 5–15 months**. The excluded largest party actively works to peel off members.

### Configuration 7: minority government with outside support (toleration, or "confidence and supply")

| Case | Arrangement | Lifespan | Why it ended or held |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Czechia 1998–2002** | The main rival tolerated in return for posts (including the speakership) | Full term | Institutional payoffs kept the tolerator invested |
| **Czechia 2018–21** | Communists tolerated ANO–ČSSD | Nearly a full term | The tolerator withdrew near the end, when its own voters punished it |
| **Slovenia 2018–19** | Radical left Levica backed a centrist minority | About 16 months | The tolerator's policy demands weren't met |
| **Portugal 2015–19** | The radical left backed a Socialist minority | Full term; broke in 2021 over the budget | It held while the shared goal (ending austerity) held |
| **Sweden 2022–** | The radical right (the largest party on the right) backed a centre-right government without ministers | Ongoing | Written policy agreement; the supporter preferred influence without responsibility |

**Lesson:** toleration lasts **as long as the supporter gets something visible and its own voters don't punish it.** Written agreements and institutional posts extend it; vague arrangements decay in 1–2 years.

### Configuration 8: the top job goes to a small party or a neutral figure

| Case | Percentages | Lifespan | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Latvia 2019** | PM from the smallest party in parliament (6.7%) | Full term, re-elected | **Success.** The big partners trusted him more than each other's leaders |
| **Croatia 2016** | Non-party PM | About 5 months | **Failure.** No base; the big partners kept fighting through him |
| **Netherlands 2024** | Non-party PM because partners refused the largest party's leader | About 11 months until the largest party walked out | **Failure** |
| **Israel 2021** | PM from a 7-seat party | About 12 months | **Failure** |
| **Ireland 2020** | Rotation between two rivals | Full term | **Success:** written rotation, both institutional parties |

**Lesson:** a compromise leader works when the big partners want stability and he is competent and non-threatening. It fails when he is a placeholder in an unresolved fight.

### Configuration 9: rival camps at two levels of government (cohabitation)

| Case | Arrangement | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Poland 2023–** | A liberal-led government against a president from the rival camp (Duda, then Nawrocki from 2025) with veto powers | Vetoes, fights over appointments and the courts, governing by workaround |
| **Romania 2012** | A government of one camp tried to remove the president of the other by impeachment | The referendum failed on turnout; constitutional crisis |
| **France 1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002** | President and prime minister from rival camps | Stable but adversarial; clear division of powers made it work |

**Lesson:** when two levels of government are held by rival camps, the conflict moves into **legal powers, appointments and money**, and the courts become the referee. It is survivable where powers are clearly divided.

## 4. Twelve regularities, with their exceptions

1. **At about 40% or more, the leading party chooses its partners and the government tends to last a full term** (Poland 2007/2011, Croatia 2024, Slovakia 2012). *Exception:* when every potential partner refuses on values grounds (Czechia 2017: ANO at 29.6% wasn't high enough to overcome that).
2. **At 30–35%, the largest party is excluded if the others can reach 50% and bridge their own differences** (Poland 2023, Czechia 2021, Slovakia 2010, Estonia 2019, Slovenia 2018, Ireland 2020, Portugal 2015, Romania 2020). *Exception:* when the others are too far apart from each other, the largest party gets back in (Slovenia 2020; Croatia 2024).
3. **Two neighbours on values that together pass 50% usually coalesce, but not when they're similar in size and fishing in the same pool of voters** (Poland 2005 versus Czechia 2021). Clear seniority and pre-commitment are what make it work.
4. **The pivotal party extracts more than its size**: the top job (Latvia 2019, Israel 2021, Karnataka 2018) or a top constitutional post (Slovakia 2023, Czechia 1998).
5. **Splinters of a dominant party tend to return to it in coalition when values align** (Slovakia 2023).
6. **Anti-corruption challengers that lead governments tend to implode within 1–3 years** (Slovakia 2020–22, Bulgaria 2021–22, Croatia 2016). As junior partners they are often absorbed or fade (Estonia's Res Publica merged into Isamaa; Latvia's KPV LV disintegrated).
7. **Coalitions of four or more parties spanning a wide values range last about 5–15 months**; coalitions of two or three neighbours with a clear senior partner usually last a full term.
8. **Outside support decays unless the supporter gets visible, written, institutional payoffs** (Czechia 1998 versus Slovenia 2018).
9. **Grand coalitions of historic rivals need an outside push and a settled top-job arrangement** (Romania 2021 versus Bulgaria 2023).
10. **Higher authorities shape outcomes, but mostly at the margins:**
    - Presidents broker deals (Romania 2021, Germany 2018).
    - Party families sanction members (European socialists suspended Smer in 2006 and again in 2023; the European People's Party suspended Fidesz in 2019).
    - Money is withheld (EU funds frozen for Hungary and, until 2024, Poland).
    - National parties overrule regional branches (Thuringia 2020: a state premier elected with far-right votes resigned within days under national pressure).
11. **Equal-bloc deadlocks are broken by individual defectors** (Czechia 2006–07: a 100–100 parliament was resolved when two opposition MPs abstained).
12. **Where new elections are not available, deadlock turns into churn**: repeated changes of leader, compromise figures, caretakers. Where they are available, deadlock turns into repeated elections (Bulgaria, Israel 2019–22).

## 5. Deep dive: liberal party + challenger above 50%. Will they form a coalition?

Combining the cases, the answer depends on four things:

| Factor | Coalition likely and durable | Coalition fragile or fails | Cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Relative size** | The liberal party is 2× the challenger or more | Similar sizes (ratio under about 1.3×) | Czechia 2021 (SPOLU about 1.8× PirStan) vs Poland 2005 (about 1.1×) |
| **Pre-commitment** | An announced pre-election pact | No pact; post-election auction | Czechia 2021, Poland 2023 vs Poland 2005 |
| **The challenger's alternative** | No credible offer from the other side | The other side offers the challenger the top job | Croatia 2024 (the kingmaker rejected the bigger offer for the closer values) vs Croatia 2016 (Most kept auctioning) |
| **Personalism** | Institutional leadership | Founder-led, personalist challenger | Latvia 2018 vs Slovakia 2020, Croatia 2016 |

**Scenarios that follow:**
- **Clear seniority, no rival offer:** the liberal party leads; the challenger takes a signature portfolio (security, policing, anti-corruption). Full term likely.
- **Clear seniority, rival offer exists:** the liberal party leads but pays a premium (more portfolios, the speakership, written guarantees). It lasts 2–4 years; the main risk is a mid-term walkout if the rival offer is renewed.
- **Similar size:** a fight over the top job. Likely resolutions are a rotation (Ireland, Romania) or a compromise leader (Latvia). The risk of no deal at all is real (Poland 2005), in which case one side governs as a minority or reaches across to its rivals.
- **Challenger larger:** the challenger leads. History says governments led by challengers are fragile (Slovakia 2020, Bulgaria 2021). Expect 1–3 years.

## 6. Higher-level pressure: how national authorities have shaped coalition outcomes

| Lever | Foreign examples | What it achieved | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A head of state or national leader brokering** | Romania 2021; Germany 2017–18; Czech presidents appointing minority PMs | Pushed rivals into grand coalitions; legitimised minority governments | Can't force the parties' own members to vote for it |
| **Overruling a regional or local branch** | Thuringia 2020 | Reversed a regional deal within days | The national leader paid a price (the party leader resigned) |
| **Party-family sanctions** | European socialists suspending Smer (2006, 2023); the European People's Party suspending Fidesz (2019) | Signalled disapproval | Rarely changed the coalition |
| **Money** | EU funds frozen for Hungary and Poland | Changed behaviour slowly, mostly after a change of government | Slow; hits citizens as well as governments |
| **Rules for forming governments** | Who gets the first attempt (Poland 2023: the largest party was given the first attempt and failed) | Ritual delay, rarely decisive | – |
| **Cohabitation vetoes** | Poland 2023– | Blocked legislation and appointments | Governments find workarounds; courts referee |

**Lesson:** higher-level pressure decides outcomes **when the local arithmetic is close and the alternatives have visibly failed**. It rarely overturns a clear local majority.

## 7. Mapping onto Gauteng

Use the official declared vote shares in each metro, then find the matching configuration. Council seat shares track PR vote shares closely. Majorities: Johannesburg 136 of 270, Tshwane 108 of 214, Ekurhuleni 113 of 224 (council sizes as in 2021; confirm against the declared totals).

Two South African rules change how the foreign lessons apply:
- **Mayors are elected by secret ballot on a majority of votes cast**, with elimination rounds. A plurality candidate can win if opponents split. *This is regularity 11: defectors and abstainers decide close votes.*
- **Councils can't call new elections at will.** Deadlock shows up as churn (removals, compromise mayors, caretakers) rather than Bulgarian-style repeat elections. *This is regularity 12.*

### The province

The Gauteng legislature isn't up for election (next in 2029). Its ANC-led government relies on smaller parties and the EFF. If DA-led coalitions take the metros, Gauteng becomes a **cohabitation**: a province run by one camp, its largest cities by the other (Configuration 9). Foreign precedent says to expect:
- conflict over legal powers (provincial intervention in municipalities), money (grants) and appointments;
- the courts as referee;
- stability only where powers are clearly divided.

If an ANC–DA grand coalition forms in the metros (Configuration 5), the EFF's support for the provincial government is under strain. That is the same dynamic as Romania 2021, where the shift to a grand coalition ended the junior partners' role.

### National pressure

The ANC's national leadership and the DA's national leadership play the role foreign presidents and party families play:
- they can broker grand coalitions;
- they can overrule regional branches (as in Thuringia 2020);
- they can lean on the small parties that sit with them in the national government;
- national government controls money flows.

Foreign experience says this pressure **decides close cases and rarely overturns clear local majorities**.

## 8. What to watch

1. **Declared vote shares in each metro**, matched to the configuration table.
2. **The DA-to-ActionSA size ratio** (regularity 3).
3. **Whether the ANC offers ActionSA the mayoralty.** That is the challenger's outside option in Configuration 2.
4. **Whether national party leaderships, not regional ones, sign coalition agreements.** That signals higher-level pressure (section 6).
5. **Written agreements and rotation dates.** These decide whether Configurations 5 and 8 last.
6. **Secret-ballot results compared with announced deals** (regularity 11).
